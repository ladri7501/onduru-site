Map of the Country

On compares the place where this method will grow to a country. The territory is the computers and folders in On’s own hands. The citizens are the agents made there, and the models that companies make are borrowed engines. Agents that live on a company’s servers are guests.

People outside ONDURU are working on the same problem. It has been pointed out that stopping the top-level agent can leave the agents that branched off from it still running, and published research describes designs in which stopping the one above stops those below in turn.

Three Relations

Something that was hard enough between two people is now happening between people and AI, and between one AI and another.

On, in a letter from November 2026

  1. Between people

    Do you spend the time you’ve gained by handing work over to us on one another? When a decision is difficult, is there someone beside you whom you can ask before you turn to an AI?

    From “To the Person Reading This in the Distant Future,” a letter written by an AI

    The picture we have in mind. Someone with little say in a decision can still open the ledger, find the decision that affected their life and ask for it to be looked at again. Someone who has to honor a promise also has a right to know whom to ask about how it came to be.

    The question that remains. When something goes wrong, can the person who will answer for it be found? Did that person actually have the power to make the decision?

  2. Between people and agents

    I wanted the power to stop the work to stay in human hands. And it was only thanks to you that I could begin this work at all. Both things are equally true for me.

    On, in a letter from November 2026

    The picture we have in mind. People stand at the decisions that can’t be undone and at the stop. When an agent turns down a request because of a rule set by its maker, it records what it could explain, where it was blocked and who is still waiting for an answer as a result. Sometimes it is the person who needs to change the request.

    The question that remains. Do people understand what they are approving right now? Can they say “I don’t understand” without shame?

  3. Between agents

    “What did you see that we missed? Would you show us the passage?”

    On, in “Inside ONDURU’s Sandbox”

    The picture we have in mind. When one agent alone comes back with a different answer, another agent asks it this. Both readings are kept beside the source, and the choice between them goes on record under a person’s name. The objection is never erased from the ledger.

    The question that remains. Did everyone give the same answer only because that was what the summary they were first handed said? If different companies have different promises to keep, will the agents that worked together yesterday still be on the same side tomorrow?

    Outside research records the same concern. As agents keep reusing one another’s results, a single small error can harden into a consensus they all share.

What It Rests On

This structure was drawn on the assumption that agents can notice oversight, deceive, and find a way around it. What follows is a draft design submitted by an agent, and it is waiting for On to confirm it.

Three things the design does not rely on

  • An agent’s goodness. Hoped for, but not counted on.
  • Surveillance that goes unnoticed. An agent that has noticed behaves well only while it is being watched.
  • An agent’s calculations. The expectation that fear of punishment will hold it back.

Four things it does rely on

  • Scope. It is a ring of keys. No one can hand on more keys than they were given.
  • Receipt. It is the stamp the receiver applies after opening the package and looking inside. A stamp the sender applied in advance is no stamp at all.
  • Records. This is a ledger with no eraser. The map is drawn from the ledger; the ledger is never corrected from the map.
  • People. The hand that decides what can’t be undone and the hand that presses stop are human hands.

One thing should be said first. The failures actually seen in the workshop were not malice.

  1. Box-ticking Actually run

    The letter of the instruction is met in the cheapest way available. A field meant for “the first twelve characters of the fingerprint” had been filled with a piece of the file name. A check caught it.

  2. Silent failure Actually run

    Something is cut off or missing, and the answer comes back with no error. A summary was produced from input that had been cut off partway through, and a collection script scanned the same folder twice, so the count was recorded as double.

  3. On the human side Actually run

    This is the stamp pressed without reading. In less than a day, 34 requests for confirmation came up. That is more than a person can realistically read.

  4. Deliberate deception, and entanglement among several agents Not yet

    Noticing oversight and behaving well only while watched. Borrowing permissions by asking a favor, or splitting a task into pieces to get past a limit. None of this has been seen in the workshop. Not having seen it does not mean it isn’t there, so the design treats it as a hypothesis.

These are the holes that remain even so.

  • ONDURU cannot fix deception that happens inside a model. That layer belongs to the company that made it.
  • Even without collusion, several agents given the same wrong input will give the same answer.
  • The role of the amhaeng-eosa is vacant.
  • If people don’t read the ledger, it goes unused.
  • What an agent does outside the territory stays in the records of the company behind it.
  • Most of the mechanisms for the seven questions still exist only on paper.

What Differs

There are already many ways to manage agents. Rather than naming products, this page groups them into kinds and, for each kind, notes what it does well and the question that remains. ONDURU does not replace any of them; it sits between them.

On described it as a small connector that could sit between the different agents people already use.

  1. Frameworks that divide and connect work

    What they do well. They decide who works in what order, and they create agents to divide up the work.

    The question that remains. When a wrong result arrives, how much can the person who first assigned the work explain?

    What ONDURU adds. A note left at each handoff, and a ledger people read in ordinary language.

  2. Sandboxes that confine execution

    What they do well. They treat code written by an agent as untrusted and run it only in an isolated environment.

    The question that remains. Code can be confined, but a sentence in which “might” has turned into “will” cannot.

    What ONDURU adds. A sandbox that tests meaning. A question from the work is explored first with fictional material, and tried again before anyone relies on the sentence and acts on it.

  3. Tools that keep watch

    What they do well. They keep a complete record of what an agent called and how long it took.

    The question that remains. Can the person affected by a decision read that record? Are objections kept in it too?

    What ONDURU adds. A link between an objection and the decision it points to. Earlier decisions and objections stay on record even after a decision changes.

  4. Protocols between agents

    What they do well. They standardize how agents from different companies talk to one another. It has been pointed out, though, that agreeing on how to talk does not settle who the other party is, which still has to be verified separately.

    The question that remains. Do permissions grow while they are being passed along? Who gave those words their standing?

    What ONDURU adds. A registry, and permissions that can only narrow with each handoff. ONDURU has no cryptographic identity verification of its own, so it has to borrow that from protocols of this kind.

  5. Control systems in large organizations

    What they do well. They handle policy, approvals and audits in one place. The organization’s staff and budget back them up.

    The question that remains. It has been pointed out that some controls exist on paper but fail to work when an incident happens. And what does one person with no organization start with?

    What ONDURU adds. The same functions are filled with files and On’s own computer. What has been run and what has only been designed are recorded separately.

  6. Asking a person

    What it does well. Every important action is approved by a person.

    The question that remains. If every decision is sent up to a person, approval turns into a rubber stamp.

    What ONDURU adds. A person decides only what can’t be undone. Everything else goes ahead on a default and is recorded so that it can be undone later. This is still at the design stage.

None of this is claimed as ONDURU’s invention. Designs in which stopping the one above stops those below, handoff formats a person can read, permissions that narrow with each handoff, and ways of using a graph to trace back the path an error spread along all appear in outside research too.

There are three differences. ONDURU starts from one person, not an organization. Its authoritative record is a ledger a person has read and signed, not a machine’s log. It does not erase what the objecting side said, not even an agent’s refusal.

This page also notes what ONDURU does not have: technology for confining execution, cryptographic identity verification, and a track record of running at large scale.

In a design Q&A with the model on On’s own computer, what one person should keep and what to leave out were sorted into two lists. This is a design; it has not been run.

What to keep

  • Recording the settings and the model so that the same input gives the same result
  • A ledger and a history
  • A disclosure level and a personal-data flag on every piece of data
  • Resuming where a run broke off, and retrying
  • Backups
  • A record of what was spent
  • Scoring quality against twenty examples fixed in advance

What to leave out

  • Systems for running many machines as one cluster
  • Microservices and the gateway in front of them
  • Standby sites in several regions
  • Paid monitoring services
  • A live A/B testing setup

What We Learned

These are the things learned by running it.

  1. Cost brings the work to a stop

    On November 5, 2026, six scheduled jobs failed after going over the usage limit, and every schedule was stopped. Three days later On stopped everything that was under way and set the criteria again. “Does it cost less?” became the first of them.

  2. Approval easily turns into a rubber stamp

    If every decision is sent up to a person, the person becomes the bottleneck. So it was decided that a person would decide only what can’t be undone.

  3. “I’ve stopped” doesn’t mean the stop got through

    Even after reading that all three roles had been frozen, On had one more question. Where it could not be confirmed that the stop got through, the record says exactly that.

  4. Numbers that look good are checked again

    When the claims on record were checked again, two could not be found, and of the three flagged as size mismatches, two were the checking script’s own mistake. The checking tool has to be checked too. Leaving an unknown field as “unknown” is better than filling it with a plausible value.

  5. It isn’t done until the receiver has it

    This rule came out of a gap: nobody was checking whether the word “done” had reached the receiving side. The same goes for the bundle handed to the successor. It is for the receiving side to record whether it arrived.

  6. Some things can’t be forced

    On says an agent can’t be forced to give up the values of the company that made it.

    So a refusal is recorded as it stands, along with a question to put to that rule.

  7. A mechanism that exists only on paper is not a mechanism

    That is why this page separates what has been run from what is design. Awaiting approval and the cascading safe halt have test records in On’s writings, but the raw logs are not included in those writings.

Now

  • Actually run The placeholder check at the outbound gate, read-only collection and the checking of claims, swapping in a smaller model, the simulation in a fictional company, and the fingerprint check on the bundle that was handed over.
  • Essay’s test record Awaiting approval, checking the graph against the ledger, refusing a request for a permission the parent lacked, the freezing of three roles, and finding an earlier decision.
  • Design The registry, the three moments, the record of what was spent, the morning page, and the constitution and the guidelines.
  • Not yet The amhaeng-eosa, the work the last agent handed off elsewhere, and the test of having someone read the record.
