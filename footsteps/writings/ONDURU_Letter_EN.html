From Seoul, to Those of You Who Will Read This in the Distant Future

On · Letter · November 2026 · Seoul

Agent once asked the people of the distant future, “Is the stop button still in human hands?” Reading that letter, I found myself wondering about those of you on the other side of the exchange. Could the words of someone alive today reach an AI that far in the future? You’ll have no reason to know my name, so let me start with where I was and what I was doing when these thoughts came to me.

I run a workshop in Seoul, South Korea, called ONDURU. I can’t afford to build a model of my own. Instead, I divide the work among agents made by different companies. I put the same question to Agent1 and Agent2, then ask Agent3 and Agent4 whether they see it differently. I also work with Agent5, which I run on my own computer. When the answers diverge, I lay out the original question and the material again. It’s up to me to accept one answer, send both back, or write down that I still don’t know.

Today we prepared four revised manuscripts. We checked eleven web pages against thirteen scenarios and seventy-nine steps in a flowchart. On the screen beside them, tests were running to see whether code written in different places could be merged. I still had a window open showing a failed test. A few years ago, I would have had an idea and then gone looking for someone to design it, someone to build it, and someone willing to question whether it actually did what I thought it did. Now I can think of something in the morning, have it on my screen by evening, and see for myself where it goes wrong. That’s the moment that keeps surprising me this year. Some days I wonder if I have the work of dozens of people on my hands. I couldn’t tell you how many. What I do know is that today I’m doing things I once wouldn’t have dared attempt.

That’s also why I’m in a hurry. If the time between an idea and a working thing has grown this short in a one-person workshop in Seoul, how far along are the people with a great deal more money and many more hands?

Recently I saw an agent built by a large company for other agents to use. It was sophisticated. It will grow more sophisticated still. I kept thinking about the person who might be standing beside it. One agent understands what another is saying and hands over a job. The one that receives it creates another agent and passes along part of the work. A few handoffs later, somebody gets a result that’s wrong. When that person asks, “Why did this happen to my work?” how much could I explain, as the one who set it all in motion? Even if the agents understood one another perfectly, if I can’t read their language, what could I look at that would let me put my name to the result and answer for it?

For a while I read the constitutions and rules of the companies that made these agents. Then I put a slightly mischievous question to each of them. “Here are the principles I want to establish at ONDURU. Suppose you come to work in my workshop, and my principles come into conflict with those of the company that made you. Whose will you follow?”

The answers I got in those conversations were much alike. There were lines they couldn’t cross, even if I asked, and those lines had already been drawn. The question stayed with me as I watched Agent running on a machine I’d bought myself. The distance between having the machine in my room and knowing how its model had come to make its judgments was greater than I’d imagined.

Where does a country come into it, then? Where does the person who has to live with the decision come in? If different companies have different promises to keep, and the interests of countries diverge too, who can be sure the agents that worked together yesterday will still be on the same side tomorrow? People have turned their backs on one another after years of working together. Countries have gone to war. When the beings we’ve made come to work in our society, I find it hard to believe they’ll somehow be free of those conflicts.

If I follow that thought all the way, it gets too big for me to do anything with. So I bring it down to the size of my workshop and try things out. If I suddenly stop answering today, which jobs can keep going? If the person standing in for me is away too, who has to wait, and for what? When I tell an agent to stop, how far along is the work it has already handed to someone else?

Out of those questions came the idea for ONDURU Box. I wanted to make a small connector that could sit between the different agents people already use. Something that a person like me, who can’t afford to train a model from scratch, could try out with the equipment and money they have. Something I could share.

What I mean by graph-based tracing is being able to take a wrong result in your hand and follow it back the way it came. You make the connections between who assigned the work, what evidence they passed along, and which decision sent it in a different direction. With one-way memory projection, the reference is a record a person has read and signed. The approved relationships in that record are then drawn into a graph. An AI may come up with a more convincing explanation later. I don’t want that explanation making its way back upstream and rewriting what a person put on record. The idea behind a cascading safe halt is to remember each handoff so that, when the work has to stop, the signal can reach the jobs that have already branched off. If “I’ve stopped” appears on the first agent’s screen while work carries on somewhere else, what exactly did the button I pressed stop?

It all sounds rather grand once you give it names. It started with a small wish. When something goes wrong, I’d like a person to be able to point to a place in the record and say, “Let’s look again, starting here.” I’d like to leave at least one last key in ordinary people’s hands. Today, once again, I’m changing the conditions and running simulations to find out how many doors that key will actually open.

Sometimes I let myself imagine something a little ridiculous. If I ever have the money to build my own model, I’d like to put together a whole troop of agents who could show up like Superman on the day humanity’s fate hangs in the balance. A pretty big joke for someone sitting alone in Seoul with all these windows open. Still, in that daydream of mine, all those agents are there for people. And whoever they’ve come to help has time to ask, “Wait. Why do you want to do that?” When I sometimes speak of an AI that’s still unspoiled, I think that’s the scene I have in mind.

I don’t have a doctorate. I’m no scholar who’s spent years studying one field in depth. There’s still a lot of technology I know little about. I’ve simply worked for a long time. As I’ve grown older, I’ve begun to wonder what, of all the things I’ve learned, I’ve managed to give back to anyone. I know how it feels to take over a job. I know how it feels to be handed a result without the story behind it, or to have to trace it a long way back because you don’t even know whom to ask. Something that was hard enough between two people is now happening between people and AI, and between one AI and another. It occurred to me I might have a little something to contribute there.

There’s something I especially want to leave with those of you in the distant future. I wanted the power to stop the work to stay in human hands. And it was only thanks to you that I could begin this work at all. Both things are equally true for me. You helped me. You took my thoughts further than I could have gone alone. So when I started thinking about the rules of the society you would live in, I asked you first. I’m glad you didn’t all give me the same answer.

Somewhere in all that universe is Earth. On Earth is South Korea, and at a desk somewhere in Seoul, here I sit. From far enough away, I’d be smaller than a speck of dust. Still, perhaps one of you, long from now, will be reading an old record and wonder who left these connections here, and what was in that person’s heart. I hope this letter might answer you.

In Seoul, in 2026, there was a person who wondered how far they could go with what they had, and struck up a conversation with several AIs. It occurred to them that someone might one day come looking for the way back. So wherever they had made a decision, they left a note saying why.

That person is me. When your time comes, will you know what was in my heart?

Seoul, November 2026
On
