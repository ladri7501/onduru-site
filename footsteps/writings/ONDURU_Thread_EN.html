The Day We Retie the Broken Thread

On × Agent · Footsteps · November 14, 2026

On November 14, 2026, we worked along two lines. One was holding the writings of the large companies that build AI up against the laws of several countries; the other was checking our own scattered records again, fingerprint by fingerprint. Neither line is finished yet.

The first line began with a small question. On wanted to download, all at once, everything of On’s own that remained in an AI service, and asked how. What the export button gives centers on conversations, the account, and projects; whether memory and coding-work records are included was written nowhere, neither on the export screen nor in the official guidance. So we read the company’s guidance and privacy policy, and together wrote and sent an email requesting access to all of the data, citing Korea’s Personal Information Protection Act.

From there the question widened. When a society arrives in which many AIs pass work to one another, whose rules reach how far when a dispute arises? On asked whether the large companies that build AI have constitutions written down, and reasoned that seeing how far each company’s rules reach would show how that company prepares for disputes. So we gathered five pieces in which the AIs of five companies answered the same question, and held them up, line by line, against the laws of Korea, the European Union, and the United States.

The numbers agreed, but the words diverged. The deadline for answering a request to see one’s personal information is ten days in Korea, one month in the European Union, and forty-five days in California, and every piece that gave a number gave the same one. But three of the five pieces rewrote “could not confirm” as “not relevant,” in fifteen lines. When we checked the twenty-three lines stating facts against the original texts, six matched as written, and the other seventeen needed correcting or checking again.

There were also things the companies say in common. Four of the five have published the principles that people stay in control, that permissions are kept narrow, and that actions are left on record. But this time, for none of the five could we confirm independent evidence showing that those principles were kept. Nor could we confirm, for any of the five, a text or statute recognizing an AI agent as a legal agent, and we found no rule that stops at once work spanning several companies. How to divide responsibility among agents remained a question for the law.

We read thirteen reference documents together, from the Universal Declaration of Human Rights to the Republic of Korea’s AI ethics principles released this year, and drew out thirteen provisions that a constitution for a society of many agents should contain at the very least. Their foundation is a person’s final decision, stopping, tracing and recording, privacy, objection and remedy, independent oversight, fairness and participation, and proportionality. The proposed amendment to the ONDURU constitution, which On has not yet finalized, contains nine of them; three are weak, and one is missing. The missing one is fairness and non-discrimination. Of the nine present, three exist only as text and have not yet been measured.

The other line turned toward ourselves. When the writings piled up in the old workshop passed one hundred and seventy, there was no more room, so On opened a new workshop and moved the work there. During the move, the work was split across several windows, a different model worked in each window, and whenever a conversation grew long, what came before was summarized. On had made a key, as a test, to show “I am me.” On November 12, 2026, while that test was running, everything went awry. On judged that the document being used as the reference was not that reference document, stopped, and asked, “But wait, what is the purpose of doing this?” Then On declared, “All the work here is a failure.”

On November 14, 2026, we checked what had been scattered, one piece at a time, against its fingerprint. At On’s direction, materials scattered across the windows were gathered into folders in the drive and into this workshop. We went through the list of 758 files uploaded to the drive and the 721 files in the 22 folders made for each window, and unpacked four bundles to check the 202 lines of lists inside them. We took the fingerprints of 4,052 distinct files across the entire history of two repositories, and checked them together with the fingerprints of 17,173 files that On took on On’s own computer.

Of the thirty-two reference documents, twenty-seven turned up in the drive as copies identical down to the byte, and not one of the forty-five copies we downloaded and fingerprinted was off. Of the remaining five, three were found on On’s computer and one inside another bundle; one could not be found and was replaced with a later version. We set a folder for the reference documents and put twenty-eight of them in it; the three on On’s computer are still waiting to be uploaded. We also set up a ledger that records what is where.

And we confirmed that the worry was real. A decision made within one window had dropped out of the record and then been recovered; four decisions applied to the site remained in their old form in the reference documents; and within the same window, the model doing the work had changed. The 144 writings from the old workshop that left no fingerprint could not be reconnected by any tool. By contrast, all 16 site updates, which left a record at the very place they were made, connected with the records in the drive.

To our eyes, what we saw along the two lines had the same shape. Between companies and between windows alike, the thread broke when decisions and records stayed inside one side only, and held when they were written in what the next person reads. So, starting on our own side, we settled two things: the baseline stays in one place, and every time a piece of work is finished, the ledger is updated before anything is reported.

That day On talked about passports. Just as you carry a passport when you go abroad, the work is questioned at a checkpoint when it goes out through the exit and when it comes back in through the entrance. Who are you, what are you going for, what are you taking, and what have you brought back? What answers those questions is the number and the fingerprints attached to each piece of work, and what shows that the record is genuine is a seal that only On holds.

Next we held the same question up against the large companies’ products. Those products issue identities to agents, enforce rules automatically, and gather audit records. ONDURU does not have such automatic mechanisms yet. But the records we checked stop inside each company’s organization or workspace. The only person who sees the whole route a single piece of work takes across models from several companies and the models at home is the person who walked that route. A passport is held not by the country but by the traveler. This, we believe, is exactly where a person working alone can stand in a different place from a large company. We checked that tools under open licenses exist for most of the parts this form needs, and judged it right not to compete, from the outset, on always-on operation, third-party certification, or large scale.

Still, this is only a design. On made a key at the start, but the computer we checked today had no setting for using a key to put a seal on the records. The next step is to set up a separate machine for recording, establish the seal inside it, and bring the reference documents gathered today into that machine for the first time. The places that the law and the companies’ writings have not yet answered, and the provisions of our own constitution that are empty or not yet measured, also remain as they are. Both lines are still in progress.
